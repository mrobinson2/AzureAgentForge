The Governed-AI Pattern Library

Nine interface patterns for consumer-facing AI, extracted as reference implementations: live below, documented inline, themeable by design tokens. New pages should compose these, not reinvent them — and real product work can treat them as spec. All example businesses and data are fictional; every interactive surface is a simulation.

Token theme (same markup, zero changes):

P1 Demo Honesty Badge

Problem: a demo that lets anyone believe a mock is live poisons trust in everything after it. The hardest rule: never imply a simulation is live.

Where it applies: every simulated surface, styled per identity.

Usage: one badge per simulated surface, permanently visible (not a tooltip, not a footnote). Name what is simulated and what production does instead. Tokens: --gov-warn.

Demonstration — simulated AI, not a live integration. Production runs live under the same governance.

P2 Trust Receipt

Problem: invisible AI work erodes trust even when correct. Owners ask "what did it say to my customer?"; customers ask "where did this number come from?"

Where it applies: quote receipts, per-message summaries, call summaries, assembly/order receipts, and privacy receipts (the pattern turned inward).

Usage: attach to every consequential AI output. Three line types: did (what happened), applied (which policy/source), deferred (where a human decides — always ≥1 or say "none"). Collapsible via <details>; keep the summary line visible. JS: none required.

P3 Refusal Card

Problem: a wrong answer in a licensed/judgment domain is a liability event. The repeated finding: the refusal is every demo's most-trusted moment.

Where it applies: out-of-area work, permit thresholds, vague deadlines, drainage/engineering calls, allergy and medical questions, and coverage/out-of-pocket questions — any place the owner hasn't priced or ruled.

Usage: three mandatory parts — the refusal (plain, unhedged), the why (a rule the reader can respect), the human path (named person + promised time). Never refuse into a dead end. Tokens: --gov-warn border.

That's not mine to answer.

Why: repair-permit thresholds vary by local authority — this is on the owner's never-answer list, and guessing here could cost you a stop-work order.

What happens instead: Robin at Fabrikam Field Services (simulated) verifies with the permitting authority and calls you back before 11 AM tomorrow. Verified answers become new cited entries.

P4 Approval Gate

Problem: owners want AI's speed but not its authority. Consequential actions must wait for a human — visibly.

Where it applies: morning briefings, at-risk-customer rescues, message queues, event follow-ups, win-back drafts (with a "skip — leave them be" option), and proposal review.

Usage: show the draft in full (never "AI has prepared a message"), then Approve / Adjust / Decline. Every outcome renders a decided-state line naming what actually happened, including "(simulated)" in demos. Init: Gov.gate(el). Try it:

Draft to customer (Fabrikam Field Services, simulated): "Quick heads-up on tomorrow — your part is on a delayed truck. We're holding two backup windows and will confirm by 8 AM."

P5 Pricing-Policy Engine

Problem: instant quotes feel made-up and owners fear being locked into an AI's bad price. The fix: a deterministic engine over the owner's written policy, showing its math, quoting ranges, deferring the final number.

Where it applies: service price books, phased estimates, per-unit pricing, and menu-style quotes.

Usage: policy = data (versioned, owner-named); output = line items + range, never a single figure; always paired with P2 (receipt) and an owner-approval step. API: Gov.quote(policy, picks). Live (three picks, watch the math):

    P6 Autonomy Policy Panel

    Problem: a single AI on/off switch forces an impossible choice. Autonomy must be per-action-type, owner-set, with an enforced "never" tier.

    Where it applies: anywhere an assistant can both answer safely and act consequentially — booking-within-policy vs. judgment calls, win-back rules, escalation-only topics.

    Usage: exactly three tiers — autonomous (reversible, ledgered), your approval (P4 gate), never — escalate (P3 card; enforced in code, not prompt). Pair with an autonomous-action ledger. Extension: an earned-autonomy ladder.

    • Answer approved FAQsautonomous
    • Send remindersautonomous
    • Send quotesyour approval
    • Reschedule a customeryour approval
    • Code / permit / medical / coverage answersnever — escalate

    P7 Sealed Record

    Problem: a quoted number that lives in a text thread is deniable by either side — the "lowball trap" trades on exactly that. Numbers that outlive the conversation need a record the customer can check.

    Where it applies: sealed quotes, change orders, and the delivery-day test — any quote a customer will hold you to later.

    Usage: seal = {id, sealed-at, basis, range, change-rules}. Append-only: re-sealing supersedes, never edits; changes are their own sealed records priced at the ORIGINAL seal's policy version. Verification is customer-facing: an ID lookup renders VERIFIED or MISMATCH with a named human path. Demos use scripted lookups — free text would imply live classification. Tokens: --gov-ok, --gov-warn.

    P8 Movement Log (queue transparency)

    Problem: opaque queues breed "any news?" calls and stale lists in both directions — customers defect silently, businesses chase blindly.

    Where it applies: waitlists (e.g. a childcare enrollment queue) and deadline-chain variants such as client-owed documents.

    Usage: position card = {position, of-N, honest-window, movement-log[], what-would-change-this}. Every movement names its cause and appears in every affected customer's log; the window is a range on purpose (an exact date is a P3 refusal); priority rules are published. Queue-hygiene messages are drafted no-pressure and gated (P4).

    P9 Signed Charter (amendment asymmetry)

    Problem: settings sprawl — five surfaces, five vendors' toggles, no artifact the owner has actually read and signed, nothing the surfaces can cite.

    Where it applies: the single governance profile that P6 summarizes, that a governance firewall enforces, and that a public disclosures page can publish.

    Usage: charter = {articles, signature, version, history[]}. The asymmetry: tighten applies instantly with no ceremony; loosen requires a fresh signature on version N+1; never-list items are not loosenable in-product (P3 → human review). Versions are never edited, only succeeded. Every surface cites its clause — that's what makes refusals respectable.
